The problem

Last-mile delivery in Lagos runs on petrol, and on uncertainty.

Riders and fleet operators face four practical problems. Each one shapes a part of the service Kivora is developing.

Unpredictable fuel expenditure

Petrol price volatility makes the monthly cost of running a delivery motorcycle hard to plan, for riders and for fleets alike.

Petrol: ₦185/litre official price (May 2023) → about ₦1,360/litre at NNPC Lagos stations (1 Oct 2026)

One monthly subscription covering vehicle, battery and energy access.

High upfront EV costs

Buying the vehicle and its battery outright is a real barrier. The battery is the most expensive and fastest-wearing part, and riders carry its degradation risk.

Battery-as-a-Service: Kivora intends to own and replace the packs.

Unreliable charging access

Dependence on an unreliable grid and very limited public charging can stop electric vehicles, and the deliveries that depend on them.

82.5% of Nigerian firms report frequent power outages (World Bank Enterprise Survey, 2025)

Solar-supported swap hubs with storage and grid or generator backup.

Downtime and limited operational data

Breakdowns, slow roadside repairs and little visibility into running costs make fleet performance hard to manage and improve.

Maintenance within the service, plus uptime and cost reporting.

Kivora does not promise fuel savings. Its offer rests on access without an upfront battery purchase, reliable energy and contracted service, and any cost comparison with petrol will be measured with each customer. Figures above are from public sources cited in Kivora's business plan.

Our solutions

Six services, designed to work as one.

Kivora is developing an integrated service that removes the battery purchase and the charging gap. These services are in development and not yet available.

Planned

Electric vehicle access

Customers access electric delivery vehicles through a monthly service model instead of necessarily buying them outright.

Vehicles are intended to be offered on lease or rent-to-own terms, and purchased in batches only against signed customer commitments.

Planned

Battery-as-a-Service

Kivora intends to retain ownership of the battery pool, so customers do not buy or replace batteries themselves.

Every pack would carry an identity on the platform and be tracked through charging, issue, return, maintenance and retirement.

Planned

Battery swapping

A planned network of swapping facilities would let riders exchange a depleted battery for a charged one along designated delivery corridors.

Hubs are planned along high-traffic delivery corridors so that swap density supports both rider convenience and unit cost.

Planned

Solar-supported energy hubs

Proposed hubs combine solar generation, energy storage and grid or generator backup to support energy availability during outages.

Each hub is planned to hold a swap cabinet, charged stock, solar generation and storage, and backup supply. Hub performance will be measured in the pilot.

Planned

Intelligent fleet software

The planned platform is designed to support billing, battery-health monitoring, swap records, vehicle telemetry, maintenance management and fleet reporting.

The first version is scoped for onboarding, billing, swap records, basic telemetry and a fleet portal. Further capabilities follow as operating data accumulate.

Planned

Enterprise energy and carbon reporting

Businesses will be able to subscribe to reporting based on operational energy and distance data, aiming to make delivery-related emissions reporting more transparent and auditable.

Energy drawn at hubs and kilometres ridden would be recorded against each vehicle and customer, so reports are based on metered operations rather than estimates.

How it works

From first conversation to measured results.

How the service is intended to operate once launched. Select a journey, then step through it.

Step 1 of 5 · Business fleets

Assess

Every conversation starts with a measured baseline of what your fleet costs today.

  • Fuel, maintenance and replacement-vehicle costs per vehicle
  • Delivery routes, daily distances and depots
  • Downtime and its effect on missed deliveries
Step 2 of 5 · Business fleets

Plan

A service proposal sets out what Kivora would deliver, and compares it openly with your baseline.

  • Number of vehicles allocated to named routes
  • Hub locations serving those routes
  • Uptime commitment and reporting requirements
Step 3 of 5 · Business fleets

Deploy

Vehicles and battery-swapping access are introduced only once the required commercial and operational conditions are met.

  • Hardware ordered against a signed commitment
  • A small initial deployment can be measured before scaling
  • Rider briefing, route mapping and portal set-up
Step 4 of 5 · Business fleets

Manage

The proposed platform and local operating partners coordinate day-to-day service.

  • Swaps, charging and faults recorded against each vehicle and pack
  • Scheduled maintenance by Kivora's operating partners
  • Replacement arrangements set out in the contract
Step 5 of 5 · Business fleets

Measure

Operating data turns into a monthly report your team can act on.

  • Uptime against the agreed service level
  • Distance travelled and energy used
  • Cost-per-kilometre metrics and incidents
Step 1 of 5 · Independent riders

Onboard

Riders are expected to join through a fleet partner, a rider association or another rider.

  • Identity and guarantor checks
  • Courier documentation (delivery only, no passengers)
  • A deposit, set out before sign-up
Step 2 of 5 · Independent riders

Collect

Riders collect their vehicle with a charged battery at a Kivora hub, with a handover and basic training.

  • Vehicle handover at a hub
  • A charged pack fitted at collection
  • Handover training on the vehicle and swapping
Step 3 of 5 · Independent riders

Swap

When the battery runs low, the rider swaps it for a charged one at a Kivora hub.

  • The rider app is planned to show the nearest hub with charged stock
  • Each swap is recorded against the vehicle and pack
  • Pricing assumes a monthly allowance of swaps
Step 4 of 5 · Independent riders

Pay

Payments are planned monthly in naira, with a weekly option to be tested.

  • The app shows swaps used and payments due
  • A clear, published payment policy
  • No separate battery purchase
Step 5 of 5 · Independent riders

Ride & maintain

Maintenance is included in the proposed service, carried out by Kivora's local partners.

  • Scheduled servicing based on distance and fault history
  • Quick repairs through partner technicians
  • A rent-to-own path under consideration

This describes the intended service. Vehicles and hubs are not yet available to book or access; the pilot depends on signed customer commitments, supplier testing and local operating arrangements.

What makes Kivora different

The difference is the combination, and who it is sold to.

Kivora's intended position is a contracted, data-backed service for business fleets, alongside a naira-priced offer for riders.

Integrated service

Kivora intends to combine vehicles, batteries, energy infrastructure, maintenance and data within a single service arrangement.

Uptime-oriented contracts

Business customers are intended to buy an agreed level of fleet service rather than simply buying or financing vehicles.

Supplier flexibility

The planned software architecture aims to reduce dependence on any one vehicle or battery manufacturer.

Software and reporting

Operational data and enterprise reporting are intended to create value beyond the physical vehicle fleet.

An established, competitive market

Electric motorcycle providers, battery-swapping businesses and vehicle-financing models already operate in Lagos, several of them well funded. Kivora did not invent battery swapping, electric motorcycles or solar charging, and does not hold patented technology. Its aim is to bring proven components together as one contracted service for business fleets, and to earn its place through service quality and data.

Business model

Recurring revenue from assets Kivora owns and operates.

Customers pay monthly for access and service. Software and reporting subscriptions add revenue that does not need new vehicles.
